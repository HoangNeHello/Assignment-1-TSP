RLS and (1 + 1) EA generally converge fastest on simple unimodal landscapes. For F1-F3, RLS and the (1+1) EA converge to the optimum more rapidly. Although the genetic algorithm (GA) eventually reaches the same optimal solution, it requires a significantly higher number of function evaluations.

For F18, all evolutionary algorithms ultimately achieve comparable fitness levels; however, the genetic algorithm demonstrates a smoother convergence trajectory and greater stability throughout the optimization process.

For F23-F25, the genetic algorithm demonstrates a clear advantage due to its use of crossover and population diversity, achieving higher best-so-far fitness values. This is particularly evident on F24, where GA outperforms all other tested methods.

We implemented a genetic algorithm with uniform crossover and 1/N mutation rate, evaluating its performance under the same evaluation budget as in Exercise 2 across seven pseudo-Boolean optimization functions.

Compared to randomized local search and the (1+1) evolutionary algorithm , our GA demonstrates competitive overall performance: it is slightly slower on F1-F3, performs comparably on F18, F23, and F25, and clearly outperforms both on F24.

These results support the hypothesis that the combination of population diversity and crossover becomes advantageous in deceptive or plateau-rich fitness landscapes, whereas single-individual EAs are more effective on simple unimodal functions.
